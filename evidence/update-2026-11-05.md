## Actual ordinary-RGB response closes one unproductive branch — E38

A six-image controlled trial changed only the owned material inputs. Both repeats and the restored-versus-original comparison were exact. Replacing the texture produced at least four channel levels of response at **24,019 pixels**, with maximum channel change 106; the tint intervention produced that response at 2,428 pixels, maximum 14. The probe texture was visibly legible on the landscape.

This establishes substantial texture influence at the tested view. Complete covering cannot alone explain a total absence of influence there. It does not establish exclusive geometry ownership, exclude partial occlusion or explain why the actual albedo remains pale and flat. The next decision should concern material transfer and visual design, rather than repeating an already contradicted complete-covering explanation.

## A green process did no capture work — E39

The asset build passed 49 checks. A subsequent capture process exited zero but selected **zero tests** and produced **zero images**: its filter retained the old method name after a source rename. Source and compiled-entry checks caught the mismatch. The successful build assets were hash-verified and reused for a separately authorized corrected capture, with no rebuild.

Lesson: bind the launch filter to the reviewed entry, then require the expected nonzero test count and actual receipts/images. Separate capture-entry failure from asset-build validity.

## Valid captures can still fail both preservation and art — E40

The corrected trial produced all **14 images**, with no invalid arms, holds or cleanup faults. Reference and candidate repeats were exact. Water masks, interior water colour and original mountain-foot interior comparisons were unchanged. The strict comparison retained **31 foreground-pixel differences**.

All 31 showed an owned-colour response in the matched four-sample comparison, with none detected at those locations in the single-sample comparison. This supports partial/background-coverage mixing. It does not establish exclusive GPU sample ownership, the absence of foreground effects, or permission to waive the original failure.

The far layer visibly changed colour but still read as pale curtain walls, flat-topped terraces and an isolated smooth mound. The nearer slopes remained patchy with a foot seam. The artwork was rejected. Source review identified a six-row ridge spanning only 60 metres and 30 capped columns as plausible contributors to wall-like faces and plateau tops; these are not uniquely proven causes.

The resulting design decision was to replace the narrow strip with broader volumes and natural endings. More texture noise or gain tuning would not directly address the dominant visible form. The new approach remains subject to actual imagery.

## Cheap counterexamples prevented an unnecessarily complex plan — E41

The next proposal incorrectly ruled out regular grids by treating a maximum vertex allowance as mandatory. For a rectangular grid:

```text
V = columns * rows
T = 2 * (columns - 1) * (rows - 1)
```

A 100×23 grid has 2,300 vertices and 4,356 triangles, below both the 2,730-vertex and 4,540-triangle caps. A 200×12 grid also fits. This does not certify a particular landform, but it disproves the argument that an adaptive triangulation is required by those caps.

An attempted coverage certificate also confused compatible intervals with established order. `ridge.low <= background.high` can pass while the true ridge weight exceeds the true background weight. Quantized synthetic counterexamples demonstrated that failure. Under the stated independent scalar-interval assumptions, `ridge.high <= background.low` establishes ordering; even that does **not** establish which multisample locations belong to either surface. The proposal was corrected to a conditional uncertainty ledger and then parked, without changing acceptance.

Other source corrections concerned perspective-dependent texel density and full allocation accounting. Average angular pixel width cannot guarantee screen resolution over a perspective image. A texture mip-chain total excludes potentially substantial encoding, decode/import, CPU/GPU and authoring buffers. Thermal erosion can redistribute material into a valley, so cap safety must be checked after the final erosion/blending and float32 conversion. These checks improved a source plan; they are not measured runtime memory or visual success.

The [synthetic helper](../scripts/planning_counterexamples.py) reproduces grid feasibility, overlapping-interval ambiguity and nonuniform perspective density using small arithmetic examples. It does not model the private render pipeline or certify coverage.

## The implementer can now see what it is trying to improve — E42

Earlier source tasks explicitly prohibited image reads. The human authorized a narrow three-image pack: the generated reference, actual control and actual candidate. The existing image-capable Read tool successfully returned visual content for all three files; request/result matching verified this independently. Filenames and captions alone were not accepted as proof of sight.

The source worker then reported image-specific defects that agreed with the supervisor's independent assessment. This removes a demonstrated feedback restriction. It does not prove how much time it saves or that the revised terrain will look good. No extra worker, MCP installation or native capture was needed just to read existing images. The tool recompressed PNG inputs to JPEG display content, so original files remain necessary for exact pixel checks. [Official Claude Code Read documentation](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/tools-reference#read-tool-behavior).

## What should have happened earlier? — E43

The local audit at 08:08 UTC recorded **74.72 combined agent-hours** associated with landscape work: 31.84 source-worker hours and 42.88 Codex supervision/review/research hours. Merging overlaps gives **63.11 clock-hours with recorded activity**, across 156.03 elapsed calendar hours. These are process/turn wall times including tools and waits, not human labour or pure inference time. Some closely related workflow/build work is included. They do not identify a measured quantity of avoidable waste.

The strongest retrospective recommendations are:

1. Verify direct visual input for the implementer at the first art iteration, and keep an independent supervisor judgment.
2. Test one broad macro-form and its visible angular window in a small actual trial before building an elaborate surface recipe. Reject a wall-like silhouette before polishing its texture.
3. Use cheap analytical counterexamples for geometry, projection and memory claims before allocating native work. Preserve tests that exercise the production transformation, rather than only helper/proxy agreement.
4. Separate collection validity, preservation, art quality and device performance. Decide the intended treatment of mixed silhouette pixels early, without deriving an exemption from the failures. A diagnostic ledger must not become an endless prerequisite to an art preview.
5. Require a decision-changing hypothesis for further diagnostics. Reuse exact assets and passing evidence when their dependencies did not change. Check launch-entry identity and actual execution counts.
6. Bundle already-authorized linked stages under clear stop conditions and keep prompts focused on the current decision. Additional tool installations and repeated planning should solve a specific blockage.

These changes plausibly shorten the loop, but no controlled trial measured a percentage reduction.
